1. What Digital Crack Gauge does

Digital Crack Gauge (DCG) measures the width of a crack, in millimetres, from an ordinary photo. You set the photo's scale, draw a short line across the crack, and DCG returns a width.

It works from a distance. You can photograph a crack you cannot reach (from the ground, across a road, from a drone) and still get a number, provided the photo has enough detail (see section 2).

How it works, in one paragraph. DCG reads how much darker the crack is than the surface on either side of your line. It repeats this across many parallel lines beside yours and combines them, which averages out texture and noise. The result is then turned into a width in one of two ways: Approach A scales it using a crack whose width you already know; Approach B estimates it without a reference, using settings tested on real cracks at many distances and with several cameras.

What it does not do

  • It does not find cracks for you. You choose where to measure.
  • It does not measure depth, and it only measures length along a traced crack (Along crack, Pro).
  • It does not judge whether a crack matters structurally. That is an engineering decision.
  • It does not replace a calibrated instrument where a contract or standard requires one. Use it to measure more cracks, more often, and from further away, and check critical ones by hand.

Lite and Pro. Lite covers the full measuring workflow: photo, scale, line, all four results and the log. Pro adds distance-based scaling with camera profiles, the formula and calibration screens, the profile graph and baseline controls, averaging options and Along crack. Pro features appear greyed in Lite.

2. Taking good photos

The single most important number is how many pixels wide the crack is in the photo. DCG shows it under every B result ("· 1.35 px").

Resolution in plain terms. DCG describes resolution as pixels per millimetre (px/mm). At 10 px/mm a 0.2 mm crack is 2 pixels wide; at 2 px/mm it is 0.4 of a pixel. You can raise px/mm by moving closer, zooming in optically, or using a camera with more megapixels.

Photo checklist

  • Face the crack square-on. An angled view shrinks the crack across one direction.
  • Get it sharp. Use a tripod or a fast shutter; blur spreads the crack and lowers its contrast.
  • Even light, no flash glare, no hard shadow across the crack.
  • Use the camera's best file: RAW converted to 16-bit TIFF or PNG, or an unedited camera JPEG. Phone photos with heavy processing (sharpening, HDR, "AI" enhancement) can read much too wide.
  • Avoid digital zoom, cropping apps and filters before measuring. DCG accepts photos up to 52 MP; crop larger ones without resizing.
  • Keep the same camera, lens and settings for a reference crack and the cracks you measure with it (Approach A).

Measuring at low resolution (down to about 0.5 px/mm)

DCG can measure cracks narrower than one pixel. It works down to about 0.5 px/mm, where one pixel covers 2 mm of surface: typical for long-range telephoto or drone photos. In lab tests a 0.3 mm line was still measured at 200 m, at about 0.44 px/mm.

The catch: when a crack is narrower than a pixel, its darkness is smeared into the surrounding pixels, so the result is less certain and tends to read too wide. DCG warns you before you measure (orange below 2 px/mm, red below 1 px/mm) and labels each result.

To get usable numbers at low resolution:

  1. Use Approach A with a reference photographed the same way. A crack or gauge of known width, at the same distance, with the same camera, lens and settings, is affected in the same way, so much of the error cancels. A reference of similar width works best.
  2. Treat B1 as an upper estimate when its label says "±30%, may read high" or "unreliable". B2 is not available below 1 px/mm.
  3. Give it more data. Use the sharpest, least processed file (16-bit if you can), more scan lines on a straight stretch, and several lines along the crack (or Along crack, Pro). Compare the spread, not one number.
  4. Confirm what matters. If a low-resolution result is near a limit you care about, photograph that crack again from closer.
3. Step by step

The four steps are shown at the top: Upload → Scale → Measure → Results. Each step tells you what to do next on or beside the photo.

Step 1 — Upload

Choose a photo, drag it in, paste it (Ctrl/Cmd+V), or use Take photo on a phone. JPEG, PNG and TIFF are accepted, including 16-bit PNG and TIFF. 16-bit photos: what you see on screen is a reduced 8-bit preview (up to 2,560 px across), but every measurement uses the full-resolution 16-bit data, so faint cracks keep their detail. Saved images of 16-bit photos are made at preview size. Check the tone setting DCG shows: leave it on the standard setting unless the file was exported in linear light.

Step 2 — Scale

DCG needs to know how many pixels make a millimetre. Pick one way:

  • Click two reference points: tap both ends of something of known length in the photo (a tape, a scale card, a bolt spacing) and type that length. Put the reference at the same distance as the crack and as long as practical.
  • Enter pixels/mm directly: if you already know it for this photo.
  • Distance + camera (Pro): type the distance to the crack and pick a saved camera profile (section 5). Planum laser photos fill the distance in for you.

If the resolution is low you will see an orange or red warning; read it, then press OK to continue (section 2).

Step 3 — Measure

Drag a line across the crack, at right angles to it, starting and ending on clean surface a little way either side. Pinch or use two fingers to zoom and pan.

  • Straighten line to crack (tick box): DCG turns your line to the best right angle after you draw it. It refuses ("Cannot fit a straight line") where the crack bends too sharply; your line is then kept as drawn. Undo puts back your own line.
  • Scan lines: how many parallel lines DCG reads beside yours. More lines average out texture but need a straighter stretch of crack. The band shown on the photo is the area being read; keep it on one straight-ish part of the crack.
  • Averaging options (Pro): how the scan lines are combined. Lite always uses aligned + median.
    • Align profiles before averaging: lines the crack up across the scan lines first. Helps when the crack wanders or your line is not quite square to it.
    • Mean: a plain average of all lines. Best on clean, even surfaces.
    • Median: takes the middle value, so odd lines (a stain, a speck, a pit) count less. Good on busy surfaces.
    • Gaussian weighted: lines close to your drawn line count more than those at the band edges. Good on curving cracks.
    • Adaptive line selection: leaves out the lines where the crack shows least clearly.
    • The defaults suit most photos. At poor resolution, try a few combinations on the same line: results that agree are more trustworthy; a large spread means the photo is near its limit.
  • Single line | Along crack (Pro): see section 6.

Press Analyze →.

Step 4 — Results

  • Cards: Approach A and Approach B, each with a switch (A1 | A2, B1 | B2) and an ⓘ for help. Section 4 explains which to use.
  • Profile graph (Pro): the brightness across your line. The shaded part is what DCG measured. Drag its edges to change it; ↺ Window puts it back. Shift and Tilt adjust the background level if a stain or shadow upsets it; ↺ resets them. Leave these alone unless the graph clearly looks wrong.
  • Name: type a name for the measurement; it goes into the log and file names.
  • ✓ Confirm locks the line and logs it. + Next line measures another line on the same photo; Next photo → moves on. Keyboard: Enter confirms, Enter again starts the next line, Shift+Enter confirms and goes to the next photo.
  • Line list: every confirmed line with its values, maximum and mean. ↺ redraws a line, ✕ deletes it.
  • Save image / Save zoomed view: the photo with your lines, labels and a strip of results. One side / Staggered and Labels / Numbers change how labels are placed.
  • Start over clears the photo and removes its lines from the log (it asks first).
4. Choosing a result: A1, A2, B1, B2

DCG always works out all four; the switch on each card only chooses which one you see. All four are kept in the log.

  • A1 — Physical reference. Uses a crack (or gauge line) of known width photographed with the same camera and settings. The most accurate result when the reference matches the crack well.
  • A2 — Physical reference, surface-normalised. Same reference as A1, but corrects for differences in surface colour (e.g. painted concrete, much darker concrete tone). Use it when the reference is on a different surface, or the photo was taken at a different distance, than the crack.
  • B1 — Empirical formula. No reference needed. Uses settings tested on real concrete cracks; a camera-specific formula profile can improve it (section 5). Reads low on paint and on coloured or dark surfaces.
  • B2 — Surface-normalised estimate. Needs no constants: compares the crack’s darkness with the surface around it. Better than B1 on paint and on coloured or dark surfaces. Needs ≥1 px/mm; least reliable on cracks under 1 px. Camera tone affects it – a B2 factor in your camera profile corrects this.

5. Calibration

There are three kinds of calibration. Only the first is needed for Approach A; the other two are optional Pro tools.

Approach A reference (Step 4, Approach A panel)

  1. Photograph a crack or gauge line whose width you know (measured with a crack microscope or a calibrated comparator card; uncalibrated cards can be off by 0.05–0.1 mm), with the camera, lens, distance and settings you will use for the real cracks.
  2. Analyse it, press Calibrate and type its width. DCG stores two coefficients, K1 (for A1) and K2 (for A2), from this one reference.
  3. Press Measure (or just go to the next line) and measure your cracks. The coefficients stay until you close the page, including after Start over.

You can also type a coefficient from an earlier calibration into the coefficient field in Measure mode. It applies to the mode shown (K1 for A1, K2 for A2). Measure the reference as precisely as you can: any error in its width carries into every A result.

Camera profiles: distance to resolution (Pro, C screen)

Teaches DCG your camera’s resolution at different distances, so later you only need the distance to the crack (or a Planum laser reading). Photograph a target of known length at several distances that cover your working range, mark its ends, and save the profile. Make one profile per camera and lens (and zoom setting).

Formula profiles and calibration points (Pro, B screen)

  • Formula profiles store B1’s constants and the B2 factor under a name, e.g. one per camera and lens. Select the right profile before measuring; it is shown in the log. Profiles can be exported and imported.
  • Calibration points fit those settings to your own camera and site. At Step 4 open “Calibrate the Approach B formula (advanced)”, measure cracks whose width you know and save each as a point. Once you have at least 8 points covering at least a 16× range of resolution, fit them on the B screen and save the result as a profile.

If in doubt, leave B on the Default profile.

6. Along crack (Pro)

Along crack measures a whole stretch of crack in one go: you trace it, DCG places evenly spaced sections across it and gives statistics along its length.

  1. At Step 3 choose Along crack. Set Sections (10–50, default 20) and the minimum section Length (default 15 mm).
  2. Drag roughly along the crack in one stroke. DCG snaps the trace to the strongest crack within about 10 mm of your stroke and shows the sections in a preview. Faint gaps up to 10 mm are bridged; longer gaps and parts off the photo are skipped.
  3. Check the preview follows the crack you meant (a darker joint nearby can take over), then press Analyze →.
  4. The cards show the mean of the used sections; a table gives mean, spread, median, min, max and 95th percentile. The bar chart shows width along the crack; tap a bar or a section on the photo to inspect it.
  5. Each section can be adjusted like a single line, or excluded. Sections are flagged (not removed) when they look like outliers, used a fallback background, or sit on a sharp bend.
  6. Confirm logs the trace as one entry plus one row per section.

Limits: one trace per photo, not mixed with single lines; the stroke must not cross itself or double back; wide cracks need longer sections (DCG lengthens them automatically). The A1/A2 and B1/B2 switches apply to the whole trace.

7. Log and exports

Every confirmed line (with Auto-log on) is saved to the measurement log, open from the L circle. Turn Auto-log off to choose what to log with “Log this measurement”.

  • The log is grouped by photo; you can add notes and delete rows.
  • Export CSV gives a spreadsheet file with one row per line (and per Along-crack section). Columns run from what most people need to the technical detail: results (A1, A2, B1, B2, active modes, accuracy, hints), photo and scale, quality flags, then the settings needed to repeat the measurement later on the same photo.
  • The log lives in this browser only. Clearing browser data, private windows, or a week without use on iPhone/iPad can wipe it. DCG reminds you weekly to export; keep the CSV files as your record.
  • Formula profiles, camera profiles and calibration points can be exported and imported as files, to back them up or move them to another device.
  • Save image and Save zoomed view give a picture of the photo with lines, labels and a results strip, for reports.
8. Limitations

DCG measures what the photo shows. Know where that breaks down:

  • Very narrow cracks in the photo. Below 1 pixel results tend to read wide; below 0.5 pixel they are unreliable (section 2). Getting more accurate results here takes experience: a matching reference, good photos, several lines and, where needed, manual adjustment of the graph window and baseline (Pro).
  • Surface colour and paint. B1 reads low on paint and on coloured or dark surfaces; use B2 or A2.
  • Blur and processing. Out-of-focus, shaken or heavily processed photos (phone sharpening, HDR, filters) read wrong, often too wide.
  • Angle. Photos taken at a steep angle shrink the crack; lines not at right angles to the crack read wide.
  • Wide, tapering cracks. The measured part can stop short of very soft edges, so wide cracks with gradual edges can read narrow. Check the graph and drag the window edges to cover the whole dip (Pro).
  • What counts as “crack”. DCG measures the dark gap. Spalled, chamfered or dirt-filled edges can make it differ from a feeler gauge or microscope reading of the lips.
  • Stains, joints, shadows. A dark mark beside the crack, or a joint inside the band, can be included. Keep the band on clean surface either side.
  • Brightness clipping. Over-exposed (pure white) or very dark photos lose information.
  • Connection. Analysis needs an internet connection; photos never leave your device (section 11).

11. Your data
  • Your photos stay on your device. Only the brightness readings along your line are sent to DCG’s analysis service, and they are not stored there.
  • The log, profiles and settings are stored in your browser, on this device only.
  • Your access code is checked each time the app opens. Access is currently limited to Australia and New Zealand.
  • Anonymous usage counts (date, country, access channel) are kept. No photos, readings or personal details.
